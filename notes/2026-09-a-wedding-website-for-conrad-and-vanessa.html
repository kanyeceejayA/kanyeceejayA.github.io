A wedding website for Conrad and Vanessa

23 September 2026

Conrad and Vanessa married on Friday 11 September 2026 at St George's Cathedral in Cape Town, with the reception that afternoon at Belair Pavilion in Paarl. Most of the people who love them live in Kampala, more than four thousand kilometres and one visa away. That distance shaped almost everything I built for them.

The site lives at conradxvanessa.com. It started as a pledge form and grew, one section at a time, into the place where guests pledged, said whether they were coming, asked for help with a visa, chose a hotel and received their invitation card. Behind it sits a small back office that the wedding committee used every day.

Pledges, the way Ugandan weddings are paid for

In Uganda a wedding is carried by a crowd. Friends and family meet, pledge what they can, and pay it off over the months before the day. Conrad and Vanessa launched theirs at a wedding meeting in Kampala on 15 May 2026, and most guests first reached the site by scanning a QR code handed out that evening.

The pledge form asks for a name, a phone number, an amount in shillings and a reminder date, with quick buttons for fifteen days, the end of the month or thirty days. A thank-you goes out on WhatsApp straight away. It falls back to SMS for Ugandan numbers that WhatsApp can't reach, and goes by email too when the guest gives one. On the reminder date, an SMS goes out on its own. The page also lists the Mobile Money codes to pay with and three committee members to call.

Every pledge comes with its own card, made from the guest's name and amount, in three photo designs and a tall one sized for a WhatsApp or Instagram story. A pledge card on someone's status is a public thank-you, and a quiet invitation to the next person.

Getting Kampala to Cape Town

A wedding abroad means a visa for most Ugandan guests. The RSVP form asks the usual things: how many are coming, yes or love from afar, a note for the couple and a preferred hotel. One switch opens the rest: passport, work, who is paying for the trip, travel dates and any earlier South African visas, plus a choice of three group trips to the visa centre in June and July. Guests could change their answers through a personal link until 4 September.

Each guest still applied for their own visa. What the site does is gather what the couple's team needs to write every invitation letter, and give the committee a table it can filter and export for the visa office. A visa help page walks through the process, the cost and the dates, with the forms to download and a checklist.

For the trip itself there are pages on the venue, flights, and six Cape Town hotels picked for the guests, filtered by budget with prices in both rand and shillings. Another page lists things to do across the five days of celebrations, from 9 to 13 September.

Invitations, one guest at a time

The invitation cards come in four designs, from light and floral to deep burgundy. Each carries the guest's name in script, the order of the day, and a QR code back to the RSVP page. The committee issues them from the back office, one guest at a time or straight from a pledge, and downloads them as images or PDFs sized for a 5 by 7 inch print or A4, or as one ZIP file for a whole batch.

The committee's side

Most of the work happened in the back office. The pledges page shows what was pledged, what was collected and what is still owed. From there the committee can search, filter, record part payments, and send reminders in bulk by SMS, WhatsApp or email. Every message the site sends lands in one log, so nobody has to wonder whether a guest heard from us.

My favourite part is the smallest. The committee already kept its pledge list in a WhatsApp group, with an emoji beside each name for paid or pending. Rather than ask anyone to retype it, the back office takes the pasted list, reads the emoji, converts pledges made in dollars, and brings them all in.

Access is by role. Admins run everything, editors keep the records up to date, and viewers can look without changing anything.

Built for the phone in your hand

Nearly every guest arrived from a phone, usually from a QR code or a link in a WhatsApp group, so every page was designed at phone width first.

Under the hood it is plain: HTML, CSS and a little JavaScript, with no framework. It began on Cloudflare Pages, with Pages Functions for the API, a D1 database, and Workers KV for admin sign-ins. Later it moved to a small server of its own, where a thin Node wrapper runs the same functions unchanged against SQLite, behind nginx. WhatsApp messages go out through a command-line tool on that server, SMS through EgoSMS, and email through Resend.

The site grew in the open. Sections went live as the wedding needed them, nine of twelve in the end, while the couple's story, the guide for the day and the guestbook stayed behind the scenes.

Congratulations, Conrad and Vanessa. May the marriage outlast the website by a very long time.
