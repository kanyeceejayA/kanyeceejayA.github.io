A week in Rome with SDMX

6 October 2025

Ivan Atwiine and I spent last week at the 10th SDMX Global Conference in Rome, hosted by the Bank of Italy. Three days of plenaries, then two days of training. The theme was "Smarter data for better insights", and the invitation had asked for exactly the kind of work we do: AI and machine learning applied to statistical pipelines, and dissemination tools that people actually use.

We went to talk about the pipeline. The paper, "From fragmentation to intelligence", describes how UBOS pulls administrative, survey and census data straight from source systems into one platform, validates it on the way in, and publishes it through the national dashboard. The rule-based checks catch the obvious; the machine learning helps with anomalies, cleaning and classifying community-level submissions. It's the plumbing behind everything else the Bureau now does with data.

What I took home

SDMX is a join key, not a file format. I had been treating it as a way to publish. The better way to see it: once two datasets share the same concepts, code lists and dimensions, a question that crosses them becomes cheap to answer. That is precisely what a chatbot over official statistics needs. Most of the effort in grounding a language model is agreeing on what an indicator means, and SDMX is that agreement, written down.

Validation can be data too. The sessions on SDMX 3.0 and VTL, the validation and transformation language, described rules as something you exchange rather than something buried in a script. We write ours in Python. Some of them should probably live next to the data instead.

Everyone's problem is fragmentation. Central banks, statistical offices, international agencies: the stories differed in scale, not in kind. The exciting demos all rested on the boring, careful part, which is people agreeing on structure. That was reassuring. It is the part we can control.

The two training days made the tooling concrete: data structure definitions, dataflows, registries. It's one thing to read the standard, another to build a dataflow with someone who has done it a hundred times and watch where they slow down.

What changed back home

We came back and started a prototype: take survey tables from the demographic and health survey and the household survey, and turn them into SDMX-ready structures. Reusable structure definitions by table shape rather than one per table, and a crosswalk of indicators so we can say quickly whether a figure exists in one survey, the other, or both. It's the first step toward cross-dataset retrieval, chatbot queries that span surveys, and briefs that write themselves from verified numbers.

Rome itself: warm, loud, and exactly as good at coffee as advertised.
